Why astronomers blink between images

A simple comparison technique helps moving and changing objects stand out from the stars that stay put.

BLINK Field Notes·Compare images taken at different times

The eye notices change

Put two photographs of a star field side by side and the fixed stars dominate both. Flip rapidly between them and a moving asteroid or comet can jump out. Astronomers once did this by hand with a blink comparator; the same simple idea still helps people inspect time-series images.

Blink uses SPHEREx exposures taken at different times. It aligns cutouts onto a common grid so the same sky position lands in the same place, then lets you step or play through the observations.

Difference images reveal motion

A second view subtracts a per-pixel median image from each exposure. The median acts like a simple reference for what stayed put. A source that shifted between frames can leave a positive and negative trace, while a variable source may brighten or fade.

That does not make every bright spot a discovery. Detector noise, imperfect alignment, cosmic rays, and bright-star artifacts can all imitate change. Blink marks candidate peaks; checking the original frames and known-object catalogs is still essential.

A useful workflow: compare the blink view, inspect the difference image, then check whether JPL’s small-body service identifies a known asteroid or comet at that time.
