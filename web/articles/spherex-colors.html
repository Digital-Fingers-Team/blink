What can 102 infrared colors tell us?

SPHEREx maps the entire sky in 102 infrared bands. Here is why that turns a picture into a source of clues about cosmic history and chemistry.

BLINK Field Notes·102 infrared bands, 0.75–5 μm

More than a prettier sky

Human eyes see only a narrow slice of light. SPHEREx observes infrared wavelengths from about 0.75 to 5 micrometers, where cool stars, dust, and molecules leave clues that visible-light pictures can miss.

Its detectors split that infrared light into 102 bands. Each band is a small window onto a different part of an object’s spectrum. Put those measurements together and astronomers can ask not only where something is, but what kinds of material and physical conditions may be present.

A spectrum for every direction

SPHEREx is designed to survey the whole sky repeatedly. That gives researchers a consistent map across the celestial sphere, with spectral information for hundreds of millions of stars and galaxies. The survey supports studies of cosmic history and the ingredients of planet-forming systems.

A color in a SPHEREx visualization is usually a translated infrared wavelength. It is a way to make invisible light visible; it does not mean the object would look that color to human eyes.

Try the bands yourself

In Blink, choose a sky-map layer such as D1 through D6, then open a target in the Time Machine. The bands cover different infrared ranges, so a feature that stands out in one may fade in another.
